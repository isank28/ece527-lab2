# Task E — Newton-Raphson Divider Error Analysis

## 1. Error Recurrence Derivation

### Newton-Raphson Iteration

The Newton-Raphson iteration for computing the reciprocal of D (i.e., 1/D) is:

$$x_{n+1} = x_n(2 - D \cdot x_n)$$

where:
- $D$ is the divisor (assumed positive, normalized to $1 \le D < 2$)
- $x_n$ is the approximation to $1/D$ at iteration $n$
- $x_0$ is the initial estimate

### Error Definition

Define the **absolute error** at iteration $n$ as:

$$\varepsilon_n = \left| x_n - \frac{1}{D} \right|$$

### Derivation

Starting from the iteration:

$$x_{n+1} = x_n(2 - D \cdot x_n)$$

Subtract $1/D$ from both sides:

$$x_{n+1} - \frac{1}{D} = x_n(2 - D \cdot x_n) - \frac{1}{D}$$

$$= 2x_n - D \cdot x_n^2 - \frac{1}{D}$$

Factor out $D$:

$$= D\left(x_n^2 - \frac{2x_n}{D} + \frac{1}{D^2}\right)$$

$$= D\left(x_n - \frac{1}{D}\right)^2$$

Taking absolute values:

$$\left| x_{n+1} - \frac{1}{D} \right| = D \cdot \left| x_n - \frac{1}{D} \right|^2$$

Therefore, the error recurrence is:

$$\boxed{\varepsilon_{n+1} = D \cdot \varepsilon_n^2}$$

### Closed-Form Solution

Unrolling the recurrence:

$$\varepsilon_1 = D \cdot \varepsilon_0^2$$

$$\varepsilon_2 = D \cdot (D \cdot \varepsilon_0^2)^2 = D^3 \cdot \varepsilon_0^4$$

$$\varepsilon_3 = D \cdot (D^3 \cdot \varepsilon_0^4)^2 = D^7 \cdot \varepsilon_0^8$$

In general:

$$\varepsilon_n = D^{(2^n - 1)} \cdot \varepsilon_0^{(2^n)}$$

## 2. Quadratic Convergence Proof

A convergent sequence is said to converge **quadratically** if there exists a constant $C > 0$ such that:

$$\varepsilon_{n+1} \le C \cdot \varepsilon_n^2$$

for all sufficiently large $n$.

From our derived recurrence:

$$\varepsilon_{n+1} = D \cdot \varepsilon_n^2$$

Here, $C = D$ (a constant independent of $n$). Since:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\varepsilon_{n+1}}{\varepsilon_n^2} = D < \infty$$

the convergence is **quadratic**.

**Interpretation:** The number of correct bits (or decimal digits) approximately **doubles** with each iteration once the error is small enough.

## 5. Discussion: Initial Estimate Quality vs. Latency and Hardware Cost

### Latency

The number of Newton-Raphson iterations directly determines the **latency** of the divider (each iteration requires a multiply-subtract-multiply sequence). Higher-quality initial estimates require fewer iterations:

| Initial Estimate Quality | Iterations Needed | Relative Latency |
|--------------------------|-------------------|------------------|
| Poor (ε₀ = 0.5) | 4 | High |
| Moderate (ε₀ = 0.1) | 3 | Medium |
| Good (ε₀ = 0.01) | 2 | Low |

Since convergence is quadratic, improving the initial estimate by one order of magnitude typically saves one iteration.

### Hardware Cost

The initial estimate is typically generated by a **lookup table (LUT)** indexed by the leading bits of $D$:

| LUT Size (entries) | Precision of x₀ | Approximate ε₀ | Hardware Cost |
|--------------------|-----------------|----------------|---------------|
| 4 (2 bits) | ~3 bits | ~0.5 | Small |
| 16 (4 bits) | ~5 bits | ~0.1 | Small-Medium |
| 256 (8 bits) | ~9 bits | ~0.01 | Medium-Large |
| 1024 (10 bits) | ~11 bits | ~0.001 | Large |

**Tradeoff:** A larger LUT gives a better initial estimate (smaller ε₀), reducing the number of NR iterations needed. However:
- LUT area grows exponentially with index bits
- LUT access time may become a bottleneck
- Each saved NR iteration saves one multiplier + adder/subtractor cycle

### Design Recommendation

For most implementations, a **4–6 bit LUT** (16–64 entries) provides a good balance:
- Initial error ε₀ ≈ 0.05–0.1
- 3 NR iterations to reach 2⁻¹⁶ precision
- Modest LUT area and access time

For ultra-low-latency designs, an 8-bit LUT (256 entries) with ε₀ ≈ 0.01 can achieve the target in just 2 iterations, at the cost of larger LUT area.
